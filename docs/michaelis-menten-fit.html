Enzyme kinetics

How to fit Michaelis-Menten kinetics (and why not with a Lineweaver-Burk plot)

You measured initial rates at a handful of substrate concentrations and now you need Km and Vmax. Many lab courses still teach the double-reciprocal plot for this. It is a good way to look at kinetic data and a poor way to estimate the parameters. This article explains why, and what to do instead.

The model

For an enzyme that follows Michaelis-Menten kinetics (Michaelis and Menten, 1913), the initial rate depends on the substrate concentration as

v = Vmax · [S] / (Km + [S])

Vmax is the rate at saturating substrate. Km is the substrate concentration at which the rate is half of Vmax.

What goes wrong with the double-reciprocal plot

Lineweaver and Burk (1934) rearranged the equation so that 1/v plotted against 1/[S] gives a straight line. The intercepts then give 1/Vmax and -1/Km.

The problem is the error. Your measurement error is roughly the same size for every rate. Taking the reciprocal turns a small error on a small rate into a very large error on 1/v. The points at the lowest substrate concentrations, which are the least precise, end up far out on the right of the plot and pull the line more than all other points together. The resulting Km and Vmax can be clearly off even when the data are good.

What to do instead: fit the curve directly

Nonlinear regression fits the hyperbola to the untransformed data. Every point keeps its original weight. This is what statistics programs and the toolkit on this site do.

In R it is three lines:

fit <- nls(v ~ Vmax * S / (Km + S), data = d,
           start = list(Vmax = max(d$v) * 1.2, Km = median(d$S)))
summary(fit)

The fit needs starting values. A Vmax a little above your highest rate and a Km in the middle of your concentration range usually work.

Reading the result

  • Estimate and standard error. The standard error says how well the data pin down the parameter. A Km of 0.85 ± 0.02 mM is well determined. A Km of 0.85 ± 0.60 mM is not.
  • 95 % confidence interval. The range of values compatible with your data. Report it, or the standard error, together with the estimate.
  • R². Of limited use for nonlinear fits. A high R² does not show that the model is right. Look at the plot.

Planning the experiment

Most bad fits are caused by the choice of concentrations, not by the mathematics.

  • Cover both sides of Km. You need points well below Km, where the rate rises almost linearly, and points well above, where it levels off. A common rule of thumb is a range from about 0.2 to 5 times Km.
  • Reach saturation. If your highest concentration is below about twice Km, the curve never bends enough and Vmax is an extrapolation.
  • Six to eight concentrations, in replicates. Fit all individual values, not the means.
  • Measure initial rates. The equation holds for the start of the reaction, while substrate depletion and product inhibition are negligible.

When the model does not fit

  • The rate drops again at high substrate. That points to substrate inhibition, which needs a different equation.
  • The curve is S-shaped. That points to cooperativity (Hill equation).
  • The points scatter evenly around a line and never bend. You are far below Km. Only the ratio Vmax/Km can be determined from such data.
